Why OptiLink

In Sri Lankan wards, doctors and patients often have to communicate without close contact, without a shared language and without a reliable network.

Three questions, answered in order: Is it a real problem? What do hospitals use today? Where does each option fall short?

The problem, on the record.

Four barriers, no low-cost offline answer.

  • Distance.

    In isolation, every close conversation exposes staff and uses a set of PPE. Masks muffle speech and hide lip movement.

  • Language.

    Sinhala, Tamil and English are all in daily hospital use, and a doctor and patient often do not share a first language.

  • Network.

    Many wards, especially in rural hospitals, have weak mobile signal or no Wi-Fi, so app-based tools fail where they are needed most.

  • Voice.

    Patients with breathing difficulty or after surgery may not speak easily, but can still press a key.

Isolation costs patients.

Isolated patients are seen less often and suffer more preventable harm.

  • Kirkland and Weinstein, Lancet 1999: health workers entered contact-isolation rooms about half as often.
  • Saint et al., Am J Infect Control 2003: doctors examined patients in contact isolation less often.
  • Morgan et al., Am J Infect Control 2009: a review of adverse outcomes linked to contact precautions.
  • Cost of each entry here: an isolation gown and gloves cost about Rs 510–630 at Sri Lankan retail prices (gown Rs 495–590, gloves a pair Rs 15–40), and an N95 mask, at Rs 430, adds to that when it is changed.

If routine questions and requests can cross the room without an entry, staff keep their distance and the patient is not left waiting.

A quarter speak mainly Tamil.

Most Sinhalese doctors rate their own Tamil as poor.

25.6%

of the population belongs to mainly Tamil-speaking communities (Census 2024; 24.6% in 2012).

82.2%

of Sinhalese doctors rate their own Tamil as poor (Ranasinghe et al. 2016, 753 doctors).

44.2%

of those doctors met second-language barriers during Medicine ward appointments.

Language barriers cause harm.

Divi et al. (Int J Qual Health Care, 2007): adverse events in patients with limited English caused physical harm in 49.1% of cases, against 29.5% for English speakers.

Interpreters help, but are rarely there.

Flores (2005) and Karliner et al. (2007) show professional interpreters improve care. In our wards they are rarely available, especially at night, and must be near the patient.

No signal. No voice.

App-based tools fail where the signal is weakest, and some patients cannot speak.

No reliable network.

Phones, tablets, video calls and online translation all depend on mobile data or Wi-Fi. Many wards, especially in rural hospitals, have weak signal or no Wi-Fi.

Patients who cannot speak.

Happ and colleagues (the SPEACS studies) describe how voicelessness in mechanically ventilated ICU patients is a major barrier to care. Patients with breathing difficulty, or after surgery, can still press a key and answer yes or no, rate pain from 0 to 10, or ask for help.

OptiLink was designed around all four barriers at once: from across the room, in each person's language, with no network, and with one key press per answer.

The problem, in one line.

Problem statement. No low-cost, offline tool lets a doctor and a patient exchange clinical questions, answers and urgent needs reliably from across the room, each in their own language.

Where the gap sits.

No product we found sits where OptiLink does: offline, low-cost and two-way in three languages.

The research gap, stated precisely.

What exists, what does not, and what is accepted in hospitals.

Existing options either need close contact, depend on a network or a smartphone, cost far too much, ignore Sinhala and Tamil, or carry no structured clinical content. We found no low-cost, offline device that lets a clinician and a patient exchange structured clinical questions, answers and urgent requests from across a room, each in their own language, with confirmed delivery and an automatic English record.

  • Local evidence of the problem.

    Ranasinghe et al. (2016); Abesooriya and Rassool (2024, 2025, University of Jaffna) on language in a government ward.

  • Local attempts so far.

    Gunawardana et al. (2019) built a Sinhala-only Android communication app, tested at Ragama Hospital: one language, one-way, a phone per patient.

  • Infrared is accepted in hospitals.

    US patent 5,561,412 uses infrared transceivers in patient rooms for nurse call. OptiLink differs: standalone, trilingual and two-way.

"We found no" describes our search, not a guarantee.

Free tools, honest answers.

Two strong alternatives are free, so we answer them on their real weaknesses.

Google Translate works in Sinhala and Tamil. Why not use it?

It is free and supports both languages, and downloaded languages can work offline.

At the bedside it needs a phone or tablet per patient, which must be disinfected, charged and kept from theft. Medical translations are not checked, a patient who cannot speak or type cannot use it, and it alerts no one and records nothing.

Cboard is free and supports Sinhala and Tamil. Why build OptiLink?

Cboard is a good one-way communication board.

It still needs a tablet per bed (Rs 24,000 or more), and has no clinician station, no alert, no reply and no record. OptiLink is two-way: the doctor sees the request at the station and the patient reads the answer in their own language.

Is it not just a remote control?

The parts are standard. What is new is the combination: phrase-code messaging so each side reads its own language, structured clinical content with an English report, and confirmed delivery, all offline and from a distance.
